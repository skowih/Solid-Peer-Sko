Part 1

💊 What does KORU actually do?

Quick take

Some people's bodies can't fight germs. They need medicine pushed under their skin every week, forever. KORU makes the pump that pushes it.

Your body makes tiny germ-fighters called antibodies. They're your immune system's army.

Some people don't make enough. Maybe they were born that way. Maybe cancer treatment wrecked it. Either way, a normal cold could put them in hospital.

The fix is real antibodies, collected from thousands of blood donors. It's thick — like honey. And they need it every single week, for life.

It used to mean a whole day at the hospital. Now they do it at home, on the sofa, in about an hour. KORU makes the pump that made that possible.

Part 2

🔬 How the pump works (the cool bit)

Quick take

It's powered by a coiled spring, like inside a tape measure. And it's safer because it's dumb, not smarter.

Here's the problem. You have to push thick medicine under someone's skin. Push too fast and it hurts — it makes a painful lump. Push too slow and you're stuck there all night.

Now do that perfectly. For an hour. Every week. By yourself.

Most companies fix this with a computer — a pump with a battery, a screen, and buttons to press. KORU fixed it with a bent piece of steel.

Two parts. That's it.

Part 1 — the spring. Inside is a steel ribbon coiled up like a tape measure. It's special: it pushes just as hard the whole way. Normal springs get weaker as they unwind. This one doesn't.

Part 2 — the tube. The tube has a tiny hole in it, made to an exact size. Small hole = slow. Bigger hole = faster.

So the nurse doesn't program anything. She just hands you the right tube. That's the whole control panel.

Why being dumb is safer

A computer pump is stubborn. It was told "push this fast," so it keeps pushing even when the body is struggling. It only stops when an alarm goes off — and by then it already hurt.

The spring pump can't be stubborn. It only ever pushes gently. So when the body pushes back, the flow quietly slows down on its own. No alarm. Nobody has to do anything.

That safety isn't a feature someone added. It's just physics. You can't copy it with better software — you'd have to start over. Then spend 30 years getting doctors to trust you.

One number that says everything: in a real study of 103 patients, 97 used KORU's stuff. 6 used the main rival's. That's not winning a market. That's being the habit.

Part 3

🖨️ The trick: money that keeps coming

Quick take

The pump is not how they make money. The tubes and needles are.

You know how a printer is cheap but the ink costs a fortune? Or a game console is sold cheap because they make it back on the games?

Same trick here.

You buy the pump once. But every single time you use it, you burn through a fresh tube and a fresh needle set. Those are KORU's parts. You can't use cheap knock-offs — a doctor has to approve them.

  • 👥60,000 people use this right now
  • 📅Most use it every week
  • ♾️Most will use it for life
  • 🔁That's roughly 3 million tube-and-needle sales a year (our estimate: 60,000 × 52 weeks)

And here's the sneaky part — it's really annoying to switch. Your doctor has to agree. A nurse has to retrain you. Your insurance has to approve it. Nobody bothers.

So the customers basically stay forever. That's the best kind of customer there is.

Think of it like pocket money. If your allowance goes up 20% but your spending only goes up 9%, you end up with more left over — and the gap gets bigger every year you keep it going.

That gap turning into real profit is the whole reason anyone would buy this stock.

Part 5

📉 So why did the price crash?

Quick take

They did better than expected — and the stock still fell 14%. Because they said one word investors hate: "later."

On 5 August, KORU reported good news. More sales than expected. More profit than expected.

The next day the price fell 13.8%.

Why? They'd promised Europe would take off in late 2026. Instead they said it'll mostly happen in 2027. A year late.

Why Europe got slow

Last year Europe was amazing (+80%). But that was one country where the government picks one winner in a big contest. Win it, and you get the whole country at once. Boom.

The other countries don't work like that. There's no contest to win. You have to convince one patient at a time. Slowly.

"In tender markets, we win the pharma practically overnight. In other markets... it may take a little bit more time."

Adam Kalbermatten, boss of KORU — 5 August 2026

In normal words: they didn't lose anything. They just ran out of the easy countries.

Here's the clue we like most. In the same announcement, they said sales would be lower — but they said profit per sale would be higher. A company in real trouble can't raise its prices. So this smells like "late," not "broken."

Part 6

🟢 Four things that look good

1 · The bosses bought shares

Four times in six weeks, KORU's bosses bought shares with their own money. Nobody sold. That includes the new boss — buying two weeks before he started.

2 · Profit per sale is climbing

Out of every $100 sold, the leftover went 62 → 63 → 65 dollars. And they signed a deal where their supplies get cheaper the more they buy.

3 · You're paying last year's price

Compared to its sales, the stock costs the same as it did 15 months ago — even though the company got bigger and better since. You get the improvement free.

4 · A free lottery ticket

Cancer treatment wrecks people's immune systems. Big drug firms are testing fixes right now — using medicines already approved on KORU's pump. If those work, KORU gets loads of new patients for free.

🐂 Why insiders buying matters so much

Anyone can say their company is great. It costs nothing. But spending your own paycheck on shares is different — you actually lose money if you're wrong. Right now they're all down on their purchase. And still nobody has sold.

Part 7

🐻 Three things that could go wrong

Read this bit properly. It's the part everyone skips and then regrets.

🐻 Bear Case #1: The warehouses might already be full

Europe grew 59%. But that growth came from shops buying pumps — not from patients using them.

Those are very different! If shops stocked up for a big year that just moved to 2027, the pumps are sitting in a warehouse. You can't sell into a full warehouse. Next report, Europe could go backwards.

Possible Debunk: Their own forecast says the rest of the year is roughly flat, not falling. And they raised their profit forecast, which you wouldn't do if you expected returns. But honestly — KORU never tells us the warehouse number. Nobody outside the company can check this. It is the biggest unknown in the whole report, and no amount of clever thinking makes it go away.

🐻 Bear Case #2: They keep saying "soon"

March: promised a number. May: repeated it. August: made it smaller. They also cancelled a cancer plan they'd promised, and Europe slipped a year.

Possible Debunk: Every miss has been about timing, never about people wanting less. Sales still grew 22%, then 20%. But if a friend promised you something three times and was late three times, you'd stop believing them too. That's what's happening to the share price.

🐻 Bear Case #3: Only 73 people work there

Seventy-three. That's about two school classes. And they've promised to do five huge things at once — Europe, a cancer plan, an approval, a brand-new pump, and a digital system.

Possible Debunk: If they were drowning, costs would be exploding. They're not — costs grew 9% while sales grew 20%. The hardest part of the new pump is already done. But 73 people is still 73 people. Something will probably be late.

🎯 What might it be worth?

Quick take

Nobody can know the future. So instead you write down several futures and guess how likely each one is.

Blend it together: $4.34

Multiply each price by its chance, then add them up. That gives $4.34, against $3.40 today. But the single number is the least interesting part. Look at the spread instead: anywhere from $1.60 to $7.25. That whole range is the honest answer.

Notice that the two worst futures add up to a 27% chance. That is not small. Anyone who shows you only the good column is selling you something.

We are not telling you this is a good buy or a bad one. The point of this whole page was to show you how the argument gets built — the reasons for, the reasons against, and the bit nobody can check. Grown-ups who do this for a living disagree with each other about KORU all the time.
